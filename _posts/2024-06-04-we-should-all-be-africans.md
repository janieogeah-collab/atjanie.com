Africans have long had a deep connection and respect for the natural world. While the rest of the world may have perceived Africans as living in “third world” poverty, the reality is that many African communities have always embraced sustainable, environmentally friendly practices as a way of life.

I come from a Nigerian family that takes pride in doing everything from scratch. My grandma, mom, and uncle have always been small-scale farmers, growing their own food and producing their own oils. Even with their white-collar jobs, they actively tended to their fields. Canned or processed goods were alien to us.

I didn’t even realise there was such a thing as canned soup. I mean, who cans soup? 😂 😂.

My grandma often substituted meat with wild-harvested mushrooms – sometimes out of necessity, and other times out of a reverence for nature and a desire to avoid waste.

And when it comes to medicinal care, she was a doctor of her own. Now this woman worked in a government hospital for thirty-two years, yet she preferred her herbs over any pill there was.

These practices, which may have once been seen as the mark of poverty, are now celebrated as environmentally conscious. Terms like “free-range” that have become marketing buzzwords in the West were simply the norm for traditional African animal husbandry. And the habit of reusing and repurposing goods, whether hand-me-down clothes (Okirika) or second-hand vehicles (Belgium), was a sustainable way of life long before the recent push for recycling and upcycling.

Only when I left my African roots and experienced the more synthetic, wasteful lifestyle of the ‘developed’ world did I truly appreciate how in tune we have always been with the natural environment.

If the rest of the world had recognised and celebrated this connection to nature, rather than disparaging it, Africans would have been free to maintain those sustainable practices instead of feeling pressure to adopt foreign, less eco-friendly ways of living.

We should all be Africans.
