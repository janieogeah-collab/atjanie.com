Africans have long had a deep connection and respect for the natural world. 

I come from a Nigerian family that takes pride in doing everything from scratch. My grandma, mom, and uncle have always been small-scale farmers, growing their own food and producing their own oils. Even with their white-collar jobs, they actively tended to their farms. Canned or processed goods were alien to us.

I didn’t realise there was such a thing as canned soup. I mean, who cans soup? 😂 😂.

My grandma often substituted meat with wild-harvested mushrooms – sometimes out of necessity, and other times out of a reverence for nature and a desire to avoid waste.

And when it comes to medicinal care, she was a doctor of her own. This is a woman who worked in a public hospital for thirty-two years, preferring her herbs over any pill there was.

These practices, once dismissed by Western societies as uncivilised, are today trendy and rebranded for Western consumers. Terms like “free-range”, today's marketing buzzword, were simply the norm for traditional African animal husbandry. And the habit of reusing and repurposing was our way of life long before sustainability became a term anyone cared about. 

How about 'circular economy' now driven by environmental guilt? Once upon a time, that was a poverty narrative built around decades of exporting massive bales of discarded clothing to African countries. Nigerians call it Okirika or bend down select. 

Oh, the double standard. 

Where the so-called civilised societies once treated Africa as a dumpsite for their unwanted goods, today a TikTok influencer filming a thrift haul is lauded for recycling and upcycling and being environmentally conscious. And although many Nigerians built thriving businesses and created circular economies for years, they were labelled by Western economists as informal and were rarely recognised as innovators, but they do the exact same thing today and are considered sustainability champions and circular economists.

Only when I left my African roots and experienced the more synthetic, wasteful lifestyle of the ‘developed’ world did I truly appreciate how in tune we have always been with the natural environment.

I miss my Africa.
